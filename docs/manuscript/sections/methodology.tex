\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}


\subsection{Solver: Transfer Matrix Method (TMM) Mechanics} \label{subsec:tmm}


The Transfer Matrix Method solves the statics of the system. We are solving the time-independent Schrödinger equation. We simulate $M \times M \times L$ domains, with $L >> M$, and we cut this into many $M \times M$ coupled slices. For a tight-binding lattice, the projection of the wave equation onto the $l$-th spatial slice yields a linear recurrence relation:

$$C_{l, l-1} \psi_{l-1} + H_l \psi_l + C_{l, l+1} \psi_{l+1} = E \psi_l$$

\begin{itemize}
    \item $\psi_l$ is an $M^2$-dimensional vector containing the quantum amplitudes for every site inside slice $l$.
    \item $H_l$ is an $M^2 \times M^2$ matrix that dictates the internal state and coupling (internal coupling within the slice) of that specific slice. For a Simple Cubic lattice, it contains the on-site energies on its diagonal and the intra-slice nearest-neighbor hoppings on its off-diagonals.
    \item $C_{l, l \pm 1}$ are the $M^2 \times M^2$ connectivity matrices governing the inter-slice coupling (hopping from slice $l$ to $l+1$ and $l-1$).
\end{itemize}

This second-order recurrence is folded into a first-order product via the transfer matrix $T_l$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix} \psi_{l+1} \\ \psi_l \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} C_{l, l+1}^{-1}(E \mathbf{I} - H_l) & -C_{l, l+1}^{-1} C_{l, l-1} \\ \mathbf{I} & \mathbf{0} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \psi_l \\ \psi_{l-1} \end{pmatrix} = T_l \begin{pmatrix} \psi_l \\ \psi_{l-1} \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

The global transmission across the system is defined by the macroscopic matrix product $\mathcal{M}_L$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}_L = \prod_{l=1}^L T_l
\end{equation}

The limiting matrix $\Gamma$, which governs the asymptotic spatial decay rates of the transmission channels, is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\Gamma = \lim_{L \to \infty} \left( \mathcal{M}_L^\dagger \mathcal{M}_L \right)^{\frac{1}{2L}}
\end{equation}
The logarithms of the eigenvalues of $\Gamma$ represent the spectrum of $2M^2$ Lyapunov exponents, $\{\gamma_i\}$. Due to the symplectic symmetry of the time-reversal invariant transfer matrix, these exponents occur in conjugate pairs $\pm \gamma_i$. The localization length $\xi$ is extracted directly from the smallest positive Lyapunov exponent, $\gamma_{\text{min}}$, which characterizes the most extended, slowest-decaying transmission channel through the quasi-1D bar:

$\xi_M = 1/\langle \gamma_{\text{min}} \rangle$
We define the dimensionless localization length $\Lambda_M$ :

\begin{equation}
\Lambda_M = \frac{\xi_M}{M} = \frac{1}{\gamma_{\text{min}} M}
\end{equation}

Within a single simulation domain $M \times M \times L$, when $L$ is large, the calculation becomes "self-averaging" along the longitudinal direction (Oseledec's theorem). We simulate many independent random disorder configurations for a specific $M \times M \times L$ geometry to extract a statistical average of the Lyapunov exponent (apparently we have to average the lyapunov exponents $\gamma_i$, not the localization length $\xi$, but idk why).  We repeat for many values of $M$. Varying the cross-section $M$ provides the different length scales required to perform the FSS with to $M \to \infty$ (although we keep M quite small bc of computational constraints, but since we are interested in phase transitions, it should work out). 

\subsection{Renormalization Group (RG) Theory and Finite-Size Scaling (FSS)} \label{subsec:rg}

RG theory says that near a critical point (phase transition), the system loses its microscopic length scales and becomes scale-invariant, governed by the diverging absolute correlation length $\xi(p)$. The foundational FSS hypothesis asserts that the dimensionless parameter $\Lambda_M = \xi_M / M$ behaves as a universal scaling function $F$, which depends exclusively on the ratio of the system width to the absolute correlation length:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_M(p, M) = F\left( \frac{M}{\xi(p)} \right) = F\left( M^{1/\nu} \chi_r(p) \right) \label{eq:fss_basic}
\end{equation}
where $\chi_r(p)$ is the relevant scaling variable behaving analytically at the critical point, $\chi_r(p) \approx c_r (p - p_q)$, and $\nu$ is the universal critical exponent \cite{Slevin1999, Ujfalusi2014}.

However, because our computational domains are highly restricted in width ($M \leq 14$) (because of numericals constraints), the finite-size data computed from the TMM contains distortions originating from irrelevant scaling operators in the Hamiltonian. To extract the true infinite-volume parameters, the basic scaling function in Eq. \ref{eq:fss_basic} must be generalized to include these finite-size corrections:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_M(p, M) = F\left( \chi_r(p) M^{1/\nu}, \chi_i(p) M^{-y} \right) \label{eq:fss_irrelevant}
\end{equation}
Here, $\chi_i(p)$ represents the irrelevant scaling variable, and the exponent $y > 0$ governs how rapidly these finite-size artifacts decay as the system width $M$ increases \cite{Slevin1999}. 

For robust numerical fitting, the non-linear scaling function $F$ in Eq. \ref{eq:fss_irrelevant} is explicitly restructured via a multidimensional Taylor expansion around the critical threshold (the Slevin-Ohtsuki polynomial expansion) \cite{Slevin2014}:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_M(p, M) = \sum_{j=0}^{n_r} \chi_r(p)^j M^{j/\nu} \left( \sum_{k=0}^{n_i} \chi_i(p)^k M^{-k y} a_{j,k} \right) \label{eq:fss_polynomial}
\end{equation}
where $a_{j,k}$ are the polynomial expansion coefficients, and the scaling variables are themselves expanded as polynomials $\chi_r(p) = \sum b_m (p - p_q)^m$ and $\chi_i(p) = \sum c_m (p - p_q)^m$. 

To secure statistical confidence, the optimization of this highly nonlinear parameter space must utilize Monte Carlo Bootstrap resampling to prevent false convergence and accurately compute the confidence intervals of $p_q$ and $\nu$.

\subsection{Considerations for the BCC Lattice} \label{subsec:mothod_bcc}

Unlike the SC lattice, where $C_{l, l+1} = -t\mathbf{I}$, the BCC lattice exhibits a complex transport topography when oriented along the $\langle 100 \rangle$ axis. There are no intra-slice bonds, meaning $H_l$ contains only diagonal $\epsilon_i$ values. All hopping is inter-slice \cite{Eilmes2008}.

Under periodic boundary conditions, the transverse momentum $\mathbf{k}_{\perp}$ is quantized: $k_{x,y} = 2\pi n_{x,y}/M$. The eigenvalues of the BCC connectivity matrix are $C_{l, l+1}(n_x, n_y) = 4t \cos(\pi n_x/M) \cos(\pi n_y/M)$. If $M$ is a multiple of 4, $n_x = M/2$ generates an eigenvalue equal to zero, making $C_{l,l+1}$ non-invertible and breaking the TMM algorithm.

A common resolution is to introduce a complex phase twist $\phi$ at the boundaries ($k_x \to (2\pi n_x + \phi)/M$) to shift the momentum grid away from the zero nodes. However, applying arbitrary complex phase shifts acts as an artificial magnetic flux, breaking Time-Reversal Symmetry. This forcibly transitions the system from the Orthogonal universality class (GOE, $\nu \approx 1.57$) into the Unitary universality class (GUE, $\nu \approx 1.43$). To preserve the Orthogonal symmetry class, the only allowed phases are $\phi=0$ (periodic) and $\phi=\pi$ (anti-periodic). For BCC TMM, the robust solution is to restrict simulations entirely to odd widths $M \in \{3, 5, 7, 9, 11\}$.

The irregular fractal geometry of the classical percolation cluster causes severe destructive phase interference, trapping the wavepacket in localized standing waves. Therefore, a much denser network is required for macroscopic quantum transport, strictly enforcing $p_q > p_c$.
