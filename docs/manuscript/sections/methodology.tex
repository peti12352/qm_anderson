\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Solver: Transfer Matrix Method (TMM) Mechanics} \label{subsec:tmm}

The Transfer Matrix Method solves the statics of the system. We are solving the time-independent Schrödinger equation. We simulate $M \times M \times L$ domains, with $L \gg M$, and we partition this into $L$ coupled slices of size $M \times M$. For a tight-binding lattice, the projection of the wave equation onto the $l$-th spatial slice yields a linear recurrence relation:
\begin{equation}
C_{l, l-1} \psi_{l-1} + H_l \psi_l + C_{l, l+1} \psi_{l+1} = E \psi_l
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\psi_l$ is an $M^2$-dimensional vector containing the quantum amplitudes for every site inside slice $l$.
    \item $H_l$ is an $M^2 \times M^2$ matrix that dictates the internal state and coupling (internal coupling within the slice) of that specific slice.
    \item $C_{l, l \pm 1}$ are the $M^2 \times M^2$ connectivity matrices governing the inter-slice coupling (hopping from slice $l$ to $l+1$ and $l-1$).
\end{itemize}

This second-order recurrence is folded into a first-order product via the transfer matrix $T_l$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix} \psi_{l+1} \\ \psi_l \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} C_{l, l+1}^{-1}(E \mathbf{I} - H_l) & -C_{l, l+1}^{-1} C_{l, l-1} \\ \mathbf{I} & \mathbf{0} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \psi_l \\ \psi_{l-1} \end{pmatrix} = T_l \begin{pmatrix} \psi_l \\ \psi_{l-1} \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

For the Simple Cubic (SC) lattice, the inter-slice connectivity matrix simplifies to $C_{l, l\pm1} = -t\mathbf{I}$, where $t$ is the hopping energy, and the transfer matrix $T_l$ simplifies to:
\begin{equation}
T_l = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{H_l - E\mathbf{I}}{t} & -\mathbf{I} \\ \mathbf{I} & \mathbf{0} \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathbf{I}$ is the identity matrix.

We study two different disorder configurations on the SC lattice:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Anderson Disorder Model:} The lattice is fully occupied. The diagonal entries of $H_l$ are the on-site potential energies $\epsilon_i$ drawn randomly from a uniform distribution $[-W/2, W/2]$. The off-diagonal entries represent nearest-neighbor hoppings $-t$ within the slice.
    \item \textbf{Site-Diluted Quantum Percolation Model:} Sites are randomly removed with probability $1-p$. For occupied sites, $\epsilon_i = 0$. For vacant sites, $\epsilon_i = V_{\text{absent}} \to \infty$, and all associated hopping terms to neighboring sites are set to $0$ to completely decouple them.
\end{enumerate}

The global transmission across the system is defined by the macroscopic matrix product $\mathcal{M}_L$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}_L = \prod_{l=1}^L T_l
\end{equation}

The limiting matrix $\Gamma$ governs the asymptotic spatial decay rates of the transmission channels:
\begin{equation}
\Gamma = \lim_{L \to \infty} \left( \mathcal{M}_L^\dagger \mathcal{M}_L \right)^{\frac{1}{2L}}
\end{equation}
The logarithms of the eigenvalues of $\Gamma$ represent the spectrum of $2M^2$ Lyapunov exponents, $\{\gamma_i\}$. Due to the symplectic symmetry of the time-reversal invariant transfer matrix, these exponents occur in conjugate pairs $\pm \gamma_i$. The localization length $\xi$ is extracted directly from the smallest positive Lyapunov exponent, $\gamma_{\text{min}}$, which characterizes the most extended, slowest-decaying transmission channel through the quasi-1D bar:

$\xi_M = 1/\langle \gamma_{\text{min}} \rangle$
We define the dimensionless localization length $\Lambda_M$ :

\begin{equation}
\Lambda_M = \frac{\xi_M}{M} = \frac{1}{\langle \gamma_{\text{min}} \rangle M}
\end{equation}

Within a single simulation domain $M \times M \times L$ of length $L \gg M$, the localization properties become "self-averaging" along the longitudinal direction (Oseledec's theorem). To reduce statistical fluctuations, we average the calculated Lyapunov exponent $\gamma_i$ over multiple independent realizations, as the Lyapunov exponent (being related to the logarithm of the transfer matrix eigenvalues) exhibits a normal distribution, unlike the localization length itself 
\footnote{that is why we average the Lyapunov exponents and not the localization lengths}
. Repeating this process for various system sizes $M$
\footnote{Although we keep M quite small because of computational constraints, but since we are interested in phase transitions, it should work out}
provides the scaling data needed to extrapolate the critical behavior in the thermodynamic limit $M \to \infty$ through finite-size scaling (FSS). 

\subsection{Renormalization Group (RG) Theory and Finite-Size Scaling (FSS)} \label{subsec:rg}

RG theory says that near a critical point (phase transition), the system loses its microscopic length scales and becomes scale-invariant, governed by the diverging absolute correlation length $\xi(p)$. The foundational FSS hypothesis asserts that the dimensionless parameter $\Lambda_M = \xi_M / M$ behaves as a universal scaling function $F$, which depends exclusively on the ratio of the system width to the absolute correlation length:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_M(x, M) = F\left( \frac{M}{\xi(x)} \right) = F\left( M^{1/\nu} \chi_r(x) \right) \label{eq:fss_basic}
\end{equation}
Here, $x$ represents the parameter driving the transition ($x=W$ for the Anderson disorder model and $x=p$ for the site-diluted quantum percolation model), $\chi_r(x)$ is the relevant scaling variable behaving analytically at the critical point, $\chi_r(x) \approx c_r (x - x_c)$ (where $x_c$ is $W_c$ or $p_q$ respectively), and $\nu$ is the universal critical exponent\cite{Slevin1999, Ujfalusi2014}.


However, because our computational domains are highly restricted in width ($M \leq 14$) (because of numericals constraints), the finite-size data computed from the TMM contains distortions originating from irrelevant scaling operators in the Hamiltonian. To extract the true infinite-volume parameters, the basic scaling function in Eq. \ref{eq:fss_basic} must be generalized to include these finite-size corrections:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_M(x, M) = F\left( \chi_r(x) M^{1/\nu}, \chi_i(x) M^{-y} \right) \label{eq:fss_irrelevant}
\end{equation}
where $\chi_i(x)$ represents the irrelevant scaling variable, and the exponent $y > 0$ governs how rapidly these finite-size artifacts decay as the system width $M$ increases.

For robust numerical fitting, the non-linear scaling function $F$ in Eq. \ref{eq:fss_irrelevant} is Taylor expanded around the critical threshold (the Slevin-Ohtsuki polynomial expansion) \cite{Slevin2014}:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_M(x, M) = \sum_{j=0}^{n_r} \chi_r(x)^j M^{j/\nu} \left( \sum_{k=0}^{n_i} \chi_i(x)^k M^{-k y} a_{j,k} \right) \label{eq:fss_polynomial}
\end{equation}
where $a_{j,k}$ are the expansion coefficients, and the scaling variables are expanded as polynomials $\chi_r(x) = \sum b_m (x - x_c)^m$ and $\chi_i(x) = \sum c_m (x - x_c)^m$.

To compute confidence intervals for $x_c$ and $\nu$, we perform Monte Carlo bootstrap resampling over the disorder realizations.
